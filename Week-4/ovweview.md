This week's field lab was the first time I got to experience digital archaeology in the field and really showed me how the tools can be used and put digital archaeology into a different perspective for me. This week I noticed a lot about this field, and as a non-archaeology student, I had a lot of fun using tools that I could also see being used in geography and showed that a lot of the digital tools being used overlap. 

We first recorded tombstones using the code sheet and KoboToolbox form on the iPads. The code sheet was way more detailed than I was expecting and showed how much work must go into the research being done. I never could have imagined how many small features we would look at on them, like shoulder shapes, font and decorative motifs that archaeologists actually have to pay attention to. 
I was definetly wasnt confidnet in every choice I made for the tombstone codes. Some stones didn't fit into the categories, and I had to guess/pick the closest match. 

Using the scanners was a really cool experience and something I haven't ever got to do in the field, and I learnt a lot of new things. A lot of this experience was very new and involved a lot of just messing around with new tools to figure out how to use them and their real-world implications. The lidar scanner was cool but was a little confusing, and using the interface on the screen was kind of hard to really tell how good the data you were collecting was. It also got lost a lot, so I needed to move a bit slower. 

I didn't fully understand how to use these tools and how they actually work, but using them made me realize how much digital archaeology is implemented with all these new modern tools that I've heard about but never actually used. It also made me realize how the technologies aren't always perfect and can cause errors while recording, like the lidar scanner, which we were told wouldn't save a scan if its file size was too big. This lab also proved that you can't trust the technology fully without experience and that you still have to think about what you are doing. 

I really liked the photogrammetry and think I will actually use Polycam for personal reasons in the future. It is also useful because it really gets you to look up close at the object being scanned, so I was paying more attention to the finer details while in person. This tool also showed me how useful it is to get a scan of an object. I found it really fun looking at the details of the tombstone fragment when I got home, even though I wasn't in the lab.

Some big overall takeaways from this week were 
- digital tools help a lot in the field but definitely have limitations, but overall are great for digital archaeology.
- recording data is more hands-on, and details matter so much more than I had previously thought about
- Talking with team members and, in this case, classmates, is really important because you can bounce ideas off of each other to help finalize decisions like choosing what features a gravestone had.
- It was also strange being in a graveyard and recording data and actually examining the locations where people are buried
- It was interesting being in a place that had over 150 people buried but only a handful of marked sites; it meant we were standing over where people were buried.
